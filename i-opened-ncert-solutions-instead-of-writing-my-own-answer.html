I Opened “NCERT Solutions” Instead of Writing My Own Answer

A student’s reflection on marks, memorisation, and why safer answers often feel more rewarding than original thinking.

I tried writing, but a few minutes later I opened Google and typed, “NCERT Solutions...”

Not because I didn't know how to write, but because this is how I score better.

I had an answer. It was simply different from what my teacher had discussed in class or what the solution websites suggested. That's when many of us begin doubting our own thinking.

Sometimes we understand the concept perfectly, yet still copy because we fear grammatical mistakes, missing points, or losing marks.

The choice becomes simple: write the structured answer that almost guarantees marks, or write your own answer and risk losing them—not because your ideas are weak, but because they don't fit the expected structure.

I had been noticing similar moments for a long time. A few days into the summer holidays, I experienced one again.

A few days into the holidays, our school assigned us an interdisciplinary project. It sounded exciting until I realised that almost everything had already been decided for us—the topic, the format, the cover page, the certificate, the acknowledgement, the index, even the research. My job was simply to copy it into twenty handwritten pages. There was no room for creativity or personal opinion.

Of course, no one forced me to do it this way. I could have chosen my own theme, added personal opinions, or argued against a point. But doing so also meant risking internal assessment grades—something that feels especially significant in Class 10, where board marks are often treated like a status symbol.

Ironically, my project topic was social issues, yet the way I was expected to complete it felt like a social issue itself.

I know I am not alone in this. Many students have their own reasoning but struggle to express it in the way examinations expect. In my case, I can usually frame my thoughts comfortably in English, but I struggle with Hindi grammar and spelling. So even when I understand the answer—as my classroom discussions often show—I still find myself relying on solution websites.

When it comes to exams, safer and more predictable answers are often rewarded.

Recently, in an SST test, I got marks cut in long answers. The teacher explained that my answers were too long and difficult to examine. I should write in short points according to the structure provided in notes.

However, the teacher herself admitted that my answer contained all the valid reasons—more than some full-mark answers, in fact. What I hadn't realised was that examinations don't assess ideas alone; they also assess how clearly those ideas are presented. Answers organised into familiar points are simply easier to examine than long paragraphs, even when both contain the same understanding.

For the past three years, I had studied Social Science by trying to understand ideas rather than memorise answer structures. I still study that way. What has changed is how I present my answers. Instead of writing long explanations, I now organise the important points more clearly so an examiner can recognise them more easily. That doesn't mean memorisation has suddenly become easy for me.

I've always found memorisation to be the hardest part. I could spend an entire day reading a textbook and understanding it, but struggle to memorise a word-to-word answer for even forty minutes.

It also made me realise that what comes naturally to one student can be difficult for another. Every student grows up in a different environment, so it isn't surprising that different subjects come naturally to different people. Many of my friends find English—especially poetry—irrelevant, while Robert Frost is one of my favourite poets. At the same time, many of them enjoy Sanskrit, a subject I struggle with. The same class that feels fascinating to one student may feel unbearable to another. And that should be alright.

Yet those differences are rarely treated as normal. Students are often judged for not being good at the same things as everyone else. Over time, they are reduced to labels like "toppers," "average," or "poor," as though a few exam performances can define their abilities.

The irony is that while newer education policies promote open-ended questions and critical thinking, students still choose safer and more predictable answers.

Sometimes this comes at the cost of hiding our own voice—especially in an age when machines can already collect information, leaving interpretation and original thought increasingly to us.

I believe memorisation should not be idolised as the final goal.

Many students are still unhappy with the subjects they study.

I still don't understand where this hierarchy came from—science at the top, commerce somewhere in the middle, and humanities for those "who can't do anything else." Even educated people casually make such remarks.

Perhaps part of the reason is that we have become used to valuing what is easiest to measure. There can be an AIR 1 in an entrance exam, but there cannot be an AIR 1 in poetry or empathy. Some forms of ability simply don't fit neatly into rankings.

There is nothing wrong with students choosing science if they genuinely want to. But often, the environment influences what they begin to value. Career choices should belong to students themselves—not to social hierarchies built around marks and rankings.

That day, I completed my Hindi notebook without using NCERT solution websites.

I probably made grammatical mistakes, but I specifically told the teacher to check it carefully because I had written it myself.

The teacher told me that very small grammatical errors rarely lead to major mark deductions.

My fear had been much greater than the reality.

I still wonder how many students are making the same choice I almost did—not because they lack ideas, but because they fear their own voice won't fit the expected answer.
