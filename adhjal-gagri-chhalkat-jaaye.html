June 17, 2026 · Approximately 9 minute read

Adhjal Gagri Chhalkat Jaaye

For years, I understood this proverb as a warning against speaking without enough knowledge—a reminder that incomplete understanding can lead to incomplete conclusions.

Sometimes, incomplete knowledge is not a sign of ignorance but the first stage of understanding. After all, everyone begins there. The real question is whether our understanding continues to grow, or whether we remain limited by what we already believe.

This question becomes especially important when we talk about young students and their place in conversations about politics, society, and public affairs. Students are often told that these topics are beyond their understanding—that they should stay away until they are older.

But is that really possible?

One day, we will become adults and cast our first votes. But until then, should students be completely separated from these conversations?

I do not think so. Students are not outside society; we are already living in a world that constantly influences us.

We hear opinions from adults, read newspapers, and scroll through reels long before we are old enough to vote. For many students, social media is the first place they encounter an issue. The problem is not exposure itself, but when a short piece of content becomes their entire understanding of a complex topic.

Many students begin forming opinions long before adulthood.

So the real question is not whether students think about these issues, but how independent thinking develops in the first place.

And perhaps the answer lies not in looking at what students know, but in observing the moment they begin to question.

I used to believe there were two types of students: the aware and the unaware. Now I think the difference is not awareness itself, but whether someone has begun connecting these issues to their own life.

Often, interest does not begin when we fully understand it. It begins when a person realises that a topic is connected to their own life. An election, a policy, or a social issue can feel distant until we recognise that these decisions shape the world we live in.

The first time I clearly understood this difference was during a class debate on women's reservation in Parliament.

The debate was not formal, and it was far from perfect. Yet, it was real.

Before that, I had never really seen my classroom as a place where students would openly discuss such topics. Since I had been following the issue through newspapers, I was able to participate.

After the debate, I realised that only a few students had actually spoken. The rest had remained silent.

But a conversation with my friend afterwards made me question my understanding of those silent students.

The first thing she told me was a point that could have strengthened our side. She had been listening carefully throughout the discussion, noticing arguments that even I had missed.

As we discussed the topic further, I realised that if she had spoken during the debate, our team might have presented an even stronger argument.

So I asked her why she had not participated.

She told me that she did not know enough to speak. She had been observing the people who were debating, but she felt she lacked the background knowledge—especially the history behind reservation in Parliament—to express an opinion publicly.

She was neither uninterested nor unaware. She had been thinking about the topic, analysing the arguments, and following the discussion just as closely as I had.

The difference was that I was willing to speak with incomplete knowledge, while she was waiting until she knew enough.

Until that moment, I had assumed that silence meant a lack of interest.

But I realised that sometimes silence is not the absence of thought.

Sometimes, it is thought that has not yet found its voice.

However, another interaction during the same debate showed me a different side of the issue.

A classmate who had not participated later told me that I should first "learn politics" before speaking about such topics.

What exactly does it mean to "learn politics" before being allowed to speak? Is there a fixed point where someone suddenly knows enough to have an opinion?

When I asked what "learning politics" actually meant, I didn't get an answer.

What stayed with me was that the fear of being judged does not only stop people from speaking; it can also make them question whether others should speak. Yet a discussion cannot begin only after everyone becomes an expert. Learning and expressing thoughts often happen together.

Later, a different conversation with another friend showed me something else.

"I really wanted to participate," she told me. "But at that time I could not think of anything to say."

The problem was not a lack of interest, but knowing where to begin. Opinions are rarely formed instantly.

The students who spoke were not encountering the topic for the first time during the debate. They had already spent time reading, observing, and processing those ideas.

Understanding develops slowly. It often begins with noticing something, questioning it, and returning to it over time.

Maybe the difference between students who speak and students who remain silent is not always knowledge.

Sometimes, it is the confidence to believe that their unfinished thoughts are still worth exploring.

This question had stayed with me long before the debate.

I remembered overhearing some of my classmates discussing politics and elections in Grade 8. Until then, I had unconsciously believed that such conversations belonged mostly to adults.

Looking back, I realised that nobody begins with completely independent thoughts. Our first ideas usually come from somewhere—our family, teachers, friends, newspapers, or even social media. The important question is what happens afterwards: do we simply repeat what we hear, or do we question it and gradually make it our own?

As a child, I was not trying to understand politics deeply. I was simply observing different perspectives around me.

With time, as my own understanding developed, my observations became more nuanced.

I began to realise something important: having an opinion does not mean having the complete truth.

An opinion can be incomplete. It can change. It can improve when we listen, question, and learn more.

When I heard my classmates discussing politics in Grade 8, what surprised me was not whether they were right or wrong.

It was that they were discussing something I had always thought I was too young to discuss.

I remember wondering:

Am I supposed to know about these things?

Am I allowed to have thoughts about topics that seem so much bigger than me?

Over the years, my understanding has changed. Many of my opinions have changed too.

And even now, I am still learning.

But this does not mean that every opinion is equally valuable simply because someone has one. Awareness alone does not guarantee empathy or wisdom. An opinion that is never questioned can become just another borrowed belief. Independent thinking is not only the ability to form opinions, but also the willingness to examine them, recognise when they may harm others, and change them when necessary.

Perhaps this is where I now understand the proverb differently.

A half-filled pot may not contain everything, but being half-filled is not a failure. It is a stage of becoming full.

The problem is not that students have incomplete knowledge. The problem begins when people refuse to learn, question, or listen despite having incomplete knowledge.

A voice that is still learning should not be silenced, but a voice that refuses empathy and reflection should not be celebrated either.

Maybe every full pot was once half-filled.

The difference was never that one was incomplete and the other was not.

The difference was that one remained open to learning.
