Beyond the Title

I was looking for readers. Somewhere along the way, I started finding them.

About a year ago, I was sitting near the front door of my house one morning, waiting for my school auto, reading A Thousand Splendid Suns.

When the auto came, I got up, still holding the book, and headed towards it. One of the younger girls in the auto, about nine or ten, tried to snatch the book from my hand.

We were friends. The auto had its own sort of friendships—different from school friendships, I think. I was the oldest among them, and the others were younger. We knew each other because we travelled together every morning.

She wanted to know what I was reading. She insisted that I allow her to read a paragraph-to prove that she was capable of reading.

I told her it wasn't really a book for her age. Somewhere in the conversation, I remembered that I had a children's book at home. I told her I would bring it the next day.

And I did.

She read it. It was a 250-page chapter book, and she finished it in two days.

After that, I gave her a Ruskin Bond anthology. Somehow, it became an exchange. Every time I gave her something, she had a book for me too. One of them had been on my TBR for a long time; it was a Sudha Murty's fiction book. I still have The Lowland by Jhumpa Lahiri with me, probably her mother's book.

That friendship has stayed with me because I don't think I was consciously trying to “promote reading” that morning.

I was just reading.

And perhaps that is what I have been doing for much longer than I realised.

At thirteen, I began writing on Quora from an anonymous account. I mostly wrote about reading and books. I don't remember having a particular plan. I just wanted to be heard for an interest that mattered to me. I wanted to communicate.

In class, I didn't really have very close friends, and my interests generally didn't match with most of the people around me for very long. So I talked to teachers too. I wanted someone to talk to about the things I was thinking about.

I was looking for readers.

Even before that, I had been trying to build reading connections around me, but I couldn't find many people.

Four years ago, I was transferred to Shajapur, a Tier-3 city where reading is not a clearly visible culture. Even getting new textbooks can be a strange experience. Books for the next academic session, which are supposed to arrive around March, can sometimes reach shops much later. Thankfully, I usually get mine by the second week of April.

A library, a bookstore, a book club—these aren't things I can simply assume will be around me.

But there are readers.

I have found them at different ages: a nine-year-old, a senior, a teacher who reads, my grandmother, for whom I might choose a Hindi poetry book from the library, my eight-year-old sister, whom I keep trying to convince that reading is fun, and my mother, whom I have literally forced to read quite a few books.

And then, in Grade 9, my librarian, who knew my passion for reading, called me and said that I was the leader of the Readers' Club.

That was more or less it.

There was no badge. No election. No interview. No announcement.

It was, in part, a formality for the record of library activities.

But the funny thing was that I had already been doing what I eventually understood as my job.

Promoting reading.

I had been doing it before anyone gave it a name.

Over the past few months, I have started thinking about what that actually means to me. I don't think it necessarily means organising an activity or telling people that they should read more. Sometimes it is much smaller.

It can be recommending a book to a friend.

One of my friends, who sits with me in class, has become interested in books partly because she sees me reading. I tell her that reading is fun. I recommend something to her. And I am genuinely proud of myself for that.

There is another classmate who doesn't read much, but I have seen him with a library book quite a few times. I make sure to ask which one it is, and whether it is a good book. Not really for a recommendation—I already have enough of those—but to remind him that he is not the only person who reads beyond the syllabus. There are people who actually admire reading. He shouldn't give up that habit.

I once heard about a literature teacher or scholar mentioning that, at a high-level meeting, teachers had been advised to keep one non-academic book in their hands when they entered a classroom.

Just carry it.

Let students see it.

I found that idea interesting because we often talk about reading as though it has to happen privately. There is also this idea of the “performative reader”—someone who talks about books, posts about them, recommends them, or makes their reading visible.

Unlike how many people on social media portray it, I actually think it is a nice thing to do.

If someone can talk about cricket all day, recommend matches and players, and call themselves a fan; if someone can recommend almost every anime in the world; if another person can call themselves a “master” of something they love—why is telling someone, “Yesterday I read this book and it was so good that I finished it in one sitting,” performative?

Perhaps reading is performative.

Because, like any other interest around us, it grows with visibility.

If something we enjoy can be visible enough to influence another person, maybe it should be.

My friend saw me reading. That girl in the auto saw me reading. My sister sees me reading.

And at thirteen, I was writing about books because I wanted someone to hear me.

My relationship with reading has changed too.

I don't read as much as I used to. There was a time when I could finish books much faster, and for a few weeks I felt guilty about reading less. But it happened gradually, and I don't feel that guilt anymore.

I read newspapers. LinkedIn posts. Medium articles. Books. Stories. Fiction. Sometimes I don't finish what I start. All of these are harder to measure as a “reading record.”

I read more slowly now.

More reflectively.

And I write too.

I don't think being a reader can be reduced to how many books you finish. At some point, reading becomes less about the number of books you can say you have read and more about what you do with what you read.

Perhaps that is why I don't mind that the thirteen-year-old version of me isn't exactly who I am anymore.

She used to read constantly and had very few people around her with whom she could talk about it.

Today, reading is no longer my entire identity. I have found a wider, and perhaps more suitable, audience. Sometimes I connect with people across the world who understand an interest that the people sitting beside me don't. Once, something I said in class that nobody around me really understood was understood by someone in the UK through the internet.

The circle became wider.

And somewhere along the way, I stopped only looking for readers.

I started noticing them.

The girl in the auto.

My friend.

A younger student.

A teacher.

My grandmother.

My sister.

My mother.

Perhaps that is what being a Readers' Club leader has finally made me realise.

The title didn't make me a reader.

It only made me notice what I had already been doing.

I have spent years wanting to find people who would understand why books mattered to me.

Now, sometimes, I am the person someone else sees holding a book.

And sometimes, that is enough to make them ask:

“What are you reading?”
